Software Design & Best Practices

Code Review Best Practices

The industry-general version of this skill. Once you've read this, Debugging Reload → What Reviewers Actually Flag shows you exactly how it plays out for real, with real quoted comments from reload's own review history.

As an author: make the reviewer's job easy

  • Keep it small. A focused, single-purpose change is reviewable in one sitting; a 2,000-line change touching five unrelated things forces a reviewer to either rubber-stamp it or spend hours on it.
  • Write a description that explains why, not just what. The diff already shows what changed — a reviewer needs the context a diff can't show: why this approach, what alternatives were considered, what it fixes.
  • Review your own diff first. Reading your own change as if you were the reviewer catches an embarrassing number of debug prints, commented-out code, and typos before anyone else has to.
  • Respond to every comment, not just the ones you agree with. "Disagree because X" is a valid response; silence isn't — it leaves the reviewer unsure whether you saw the comment at all.

As a reviewer: what to actually look for, in priority order

  1. Correctness first. Does it do what it claims to do? Are there edge cases (empty input, null, the boundary values) that aren't handled?
  2. Safety second. Could this break something else? Does it handle errors sensibly? Is anything sensitive (a secret, an unvalidated input) handled carelessly?
  3. Consistency third. Does it follow the codebase's existing conventions — naming, error handling, structure — well enough that it doesn't stand out as foreign?
  4. Style last, and lightly. Nitpicks about formatting matter far less than the three above — and many of them are better caught by an automated linter/formatter than by a human's attention.
Phrase feedback as a question or observation, not a command "Why does this catch Exception broadly instead of the specific type?" invites explanation and rarely feels adversarial. "Don't catch Exception broadly" reads as a command and can put the author on the defensive even when the underlying concern is identical. The content of good feedback and bad feedback is often the same — the delivery is what differs.

Don't take review comments personally — including as an author

A review comment is about the code, not about you. This runs both ways: as a reviewer, critique the diff, not the person; as an author, a comment pointing out a real problem is the system working as intended, not a judgment of your ability. The goal of review is better code shipping, not proving anyone right or wrong.

Revision count alone isn't a quality signal It's tempting to treat "this MR needed 10 rounds of revision" as evidence of poor initial work. As you'll see in the real data in What Reviewers Actually Flag, high revision counts in a real project often reflect evolving requirements discovered mid-review, not repeated rejection of the same mistake. Look at what actually changed between revisions before drawing a conclusion from the count.

Check your understanding

As a reviewer, you notice both a genuine null-reference risk and a minor spacing inconsistency in the same diff. What's the right priority?

  • Flag the spacing first — style issues are quick to fix and should be cleared before anything else.
  • Flag the null-reference risk as the priority — it's a correctness/safety issue, well above a cosmetic nitpick.
  • Only mention the spacing issue, since it's less likely to start an argument.
